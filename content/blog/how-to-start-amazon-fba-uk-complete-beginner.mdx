If you are asking how to start Amazon FBA UK, the short answer is this: you set up a Seller Central account, choose something to sell, send that stock to an Amazon warehouse, and let Amazon handle the storage, picking, packing, delivery and customer service while you manage the listing and the stock. You keep whatever is left once Amazon's fees and your product costs are paid. That is the whole model. There is no secret beyond it, and there is no version of it that works without you buying real stock, writing a real listing and watching what the numbers actually do once orders come in.

If you have never sold online before, have a few hundred pounds saved, and you are not sure whether this is a genuine side business or just another online scheme, the honest answer is that FBA is a real retail business with real risk. It can be run alongside a 9-5. It is not passive, and it does not run itself. What follows is the order most beginners should actually go through, the checks to make before spending money, and the mistakes that cost people their first batch of stock.

## How Amazon FBA actually works

Fulfilment by Amazon is a logistics service, not a business model on its own. You are still the retailer: you decide what to sell, you pay for it, and you are responsible for the listing being accurate and the product being safe and compliant. Amazon's part is storage and fulfilment: once your stock arrives at one of its warehouses, Amazon stores it, picks and packs each order, ships it, and deals with the customer service and returns that come with normal retail.

The money works like this: a customer pays the price on the listing, Amazon takes its referral fee and fulfilment fee (the exact figures change, so always check the current ones on Amazon's own fee pages or in Seller Central rather than relying on a number from a blog post, including this one), and what is left after those fees and your product cost is your margin. Storage fees, inbound shipping and any advertising spend come off that too. None of this is hidden: it is all visible in Seller Central once you are set up, which is why checking the real numbers on a specific product before you commit money matters more than any general rule of thumb.

## How to start Amazon FBA UK: the steps in order

Most beginners either skip steps or do them in the wrong order, which is how people end up with stock they cannot sell profitably. A sensible sequence looks like this.

1. **Open a seller account.** You will need your business or personal details, a bank account, and identity documents for Amazon's verification checks. Decide early whether you are trading as an individual or a limited company, because that affects your tax position and is worth a conversation with an accountant or a look at GOV.UK rather than a guess.
2. **Learn the fee structure before you choose a product**, not after. Referral fees vary by category, and fulfilment fees depend on size and weight. A product that looks cheap to buy can be expensive to sell if it is bulky or heavy.
3. **Research a first product on a small scale.** For a true beginner, this usually means bundling a small number of items sourced from wholesalers, online directories or shops rather than inventing a new product from scratch. A bundle or multi-pack can often be listed under a generic brand using a GTIN exemption, without needing a barcode for every unit, while you learn the mechanics before committing to building your own brand.
4. **Buy a small test batch, not a large one.** The point of the first order is to find out whether the listing sells and what the real margin looks like once all the fees are accounted for, not to prove you were right about the idea.
5. **Create the listing properly**, with accurate images, an honest title and description, and all the labels, warnings and safety information the product category requires.
6. **Prepare and send the shipment**, following Amazon's packaging, labelling and heavy-package rules, and track it through to the warehouse.
7. **Watch the first few weeks of sales and reviews**, then decide whether to reorder, adjust the listing, or move on to something else.

This is the lean approach: validate small, then scale only what is actually selling. It is slower than buying a large first order on a hunch, but it is the difference between losing a few hundred pounds learning a lesson and losing a lot more. ## What to check before you spend real money

Before placing any stock order, check these numbers rather than trusting a gut feeling:

- **The current referral fee and fulfilment fee** for the exact category and size tier your product falls into, taken from Seller Central or Amazon's fee pages, not an old blog post.
- **Storage costs**, particularly if the product is bulky, seasonal, or likely to sit in a warehouse for a while.
- **Inbound shipping cost** to get the stock from the supplier to the Amazon warehouse, including any courier or pallet charges.
- **Minimum order quantities** from suppliers, and whether you can genuinely afford a small test batch rather than being forced into a large one.
- **Any category gating or brand approval requirements.** Some categories and some branded products need approval or supplier invoices before you can list them: check this in Seller Central Help before you buy stock, not after.
- **Your VAT position.** General VAT obligations for online selling are covered on GOV.UK and worth discussing with an accountant; this is not something to guess at from a forum post.

If the numbers only work on an optimistic sales volume, they do not work. Build your first decision around the worst realistic case, not the best one.

## Common mistakes beginners make

- **Buying too much stock too soon.** A large first order feels efficient, but if the product does not sell as expected, it ties up money and warehouse space with no way to test and adjust quickly.
- **Guessing at fees instead of checking them.** Fee structures change, and categories differ; what applied to someone else's product may not apply to yours.
- **Ignoring category restrictions.** Trying to list a gated product or a branded item without the right approval or supplier documentation is a fast way to get a listing suppressed or an account flagged.
- **Copying another seller's images or brand name.** Using another brand's trademark, images or claims is a policy breach, not a shortcut, and risks suspension.
- **Treating the account as passive.** Suppression notices, PPC reports and reorders need attention. This is a business run alongside other commitments, not one that runs itself in the background.
- **Skipping the research stage because stock feels urgent.** A rushed first product is usually the one that teaches the most expensive lesson.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much money do I need to start Amazon FBA in the UK?

There is no fixed figure, because it depends on the product, the order quantity and the category fees involved. A sensible approach is to size your first test batch to what you can afford to lose while you learn, rather than stretching to the largest order a supplier will allow.

### Do I need a limited company to sell on Amazon UK?

No, you can start as an individual seller, though many people move to a limited company as the business grows, largely for tax and liability reasons. This is a decision to make with an accountant or by reading the guidance on GOV.UK, not something to decide from a blog post.

### Can I sell well-known branded products on Amazon FBA?

Sometimes, but many brands and categories require approval or invoices from an authorised supplier before you can list them, and some categories are gated entirely. Check the specific requirements in Seller Central Help before you buy any branded stock.

### Is Amazon FBA passive once it is set up?

No. Amazon handles storage, picking, packing, delivery and customer service, but you are still responsible for the listing, reordering stock, handling suppression notices, reading advertising reports and adjusting pricing. It can run alongside other work, but it needs ongoing attention.

### What should my first product actually be?

Many beginners start with a bundle or multi-pack built from products found through wholesalers, online directories or physical shops, listed under a generic brand rather than an established one. This keeps the first test simple and avoids the approval hurdles that come with reselling recognised brands.
