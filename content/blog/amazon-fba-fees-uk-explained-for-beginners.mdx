If you're trying to work out whether Amazon FBA is worth it, the honest answer is: it depends on your costs, and most beginners underestimate them. This is Amazon FBA fees UK explained properly, by type rather than by exact figure, because Amazon changes its fees and any number printed here could be wrong by the time you read it. What matters is understanding the categories of cost so you can check the current figures yourself in Seller Central and work out, before you spend a penny on stock, whether a product actually leaves enough behind.

Many people hear about FBA, imagine it's a way to make quick money, and skip straight to looking for a product. Then they're surprised when the numbers don't add up. The fees aren't hidden, but they are spread across several different charges, and if you only look at one of them you'll get the wrong picture. Go through each type properly and the picture gets a lot clearer.

## Amazon FBA fees UK explained by type

There are four main cost categories every FBA seller in the UK needs to understand before buying stock. None of the figures below are quoted here, because they change and you need to check them yourself, but knowing what each one covers means you'll know exactly where to look.

**The seller plan subscription.** Amazon offers different account plans in the UK, and the right one depends on how many items you expect to sell per month. One plan charges a fee per item sold with no monthly cost, the other charges a flat monthly subscription regardless of volume. Check the current terms and thresholds on Amazon's own seller plan pages before you pick one, and don't assume the higher-volume plan is automatically better. For someone testing their first product, the lower-commitment option is usually the sensible start.

**Referral fees.** This is a percentage Amazon takes on every sale, and the percentage varies by product category. A referral fee on a kitchen item will differ from one on a toy or a book. You'll find the current rate for your category on Amazon's referral fee page, and it's worth checking before you commit to a product, not after you've already ordered stock.

**FBA fulfilment fees.** This covers Amazon picking, packing and shipping your item once an order comes in. It's based on the size and weight of the unit, so a small, light bundle costs less to fulfil than a large or heavy one. This is where a lot of beginners get caught out with bundles: adding a third or fourth item to a multi-pack can tip it into a higher size band, and the fulfilment fee jumps more than the extra unit cost justifies. Always check the dimensions and weight of the finished, boxed bundle, not just the individual items.

**Storage fees.** Amazon charges for the space your stock takes up in its warehouses, calculated by volume and how long it sits there. Storage costs more during busier periods of the year, and stock that sits for a long time without selling can start costing more than it's worth. This is one of the strongest arguments for the lean approach: send in a small test batch first, see whether it actually sells, and only commit to a larger shipment once you know the demand is real.

## Costs that are optional, but worth planning for anyway

Not every cost is charged automatically by Amazon on every sale. Some are things you choose to spend on, but if you're serious about selling you should plan for them from day one rather than being surprised later.

**Advertising.** Pay-per-click campaigns inside Amazon are optional, but most sellers use them, especially when launching a new listing with no sales history. Advertising spend is entirely within your control: you set the budget and the bids, and you can pause a campaign that isn't working. Treat it as a cost to test and measure, not a fixed overhead you have to absorb no matter what.

**VAT.** If you're trading above the relevant threshold, or you choose to register voluntarily, VAT becomes part of your pricing and your paperwork. This article won't state the threshold or the rate, because both are set by HMRC and can change: check the current position on GOV.UK or speak to an accountant before you assume VAT doesn't apply to you. Getting this wrong isn't a small admin slip, it's the kind of thing that catches out sellers who scale up faster than their paperwork.

## How to work out if a product leaves enough after costs

This is the calculation that actually matters, and it's the one beginners skip because it feels like extra work before you've even found a product. Do it properly and you'll avoid buying stock that looks exciting but barely breaks even.

1. Start with your intended selling price on Amazon.co.uk.
2. Subtract the referral fee for that category, using the current rate from Amazon's fee pages.
3. Subtract the FBA fulfilment fee based on the actual boxed size and weight of your unit, not an estimate.
4. Subtract what you paid your supplier per unit, including any delivery cost to get the stock to you or to Amazon.
5. Subtract a realistic allowance for storage, based on how long you expect the stock to take to sell.
6. Subtract an allowance for advertising spend, especially for the first few months of a new listing.
7. Add VAT into your pricing and costs if it applies to you.

What's left is what the product actually leaves you, per unit, before you've bought a single item. Amazon's own revenue calculator in Seller Central will do much of this arithmetic for you once you've got real figures from a supplier, and it's worth running every product idea through it before you commit. If the margin looks thin on paper, it won't improve once you're paying real shipping costs and dealing with returns.

## Common mistakes beginners make with FBA costs

- **Pricing from a rough guess at Amazon's cut**, rather than checking the actual referral fee for that specific category.
- **Guessing fulfilment fees from the unsealed product**, rather than the finished, polybagged or boxed unit that Amazon will actually measure.
- **Forgetting storage entirely**, then being surprised months later by a shipment that's sold slowly and racked up charges the whole time.
- **Treating advertising as free marketing**, then launching with no budget set aside and wondering why the listing has no visibility.
- **Ignoring VAT until it's a problem**, instead of checking the current position with HMRC before sales start building up.
- **Buying a large first order to get a better unit price**, before confirming the product sells at all. The lean approach exists precisely to avoid this: a small test batch tells you the truth about demand before you commit real money to stock sitting in a warehouse.

## What to do next

Before you place a stock order, run through every cost type above for that specific product, using the current figures from Amazon's fee pages and the revenue calculator, not numbers you've seen quoted somewhere else. If the margin holds up after a realistic allowance for advertising and storage, that's a product worth testing with a small batch. If it doesn't, it's better to find that out now than after the stock has landed.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the main Amazon FBA fees in the UK?

The core costs are the seller plan subscription, a referral fee charged as a percentage of each sale, an FBA fulfilment fee based on the size and weight of your product, and storage fees for stock held in Amazon's warehouses. On top of those, most sellers also spend on advertising, and VAT may apply depending on your situation. Check current rates on Amazon's fee pages rather than relying on figures from elsewhere, since they change over time.

### How do I check the exact fees for my product?

Amazon's revenue calculator inside Seller Central lets you enter a product's price, dimensions, weight and category, and it will estimate the referral fee and fulfilment fee for that specific item. Use real figures from your supplier and the actual boxed dimensions, not rough estimates, and you'll get a far more honest picture of what a product leaves you.

### Is Amazon FBA worth it after all the fees?

That depends entirely on the product, the margin after every cost type, and how much work you put into testing and managing the listing. There's no fixed answer that applies to every product or every seller, which is exactly why working through the full cost calculation before buying stock matters more than any general claim about FBA being worth it or not.

### Do I need to register for VAT to sell on Amazon?

It depends on your turnover and circumstances, and the current thresholds and rules sit with HMRC, not Amazon. Check GOV.UK or speak to an accountant before you assume VAT either does or doesn't apply to you, and revisit the question as your sales grow.

### How much stock should a beginner buy first?

A small test batch, rather than a large order chasing a better unit price, is the safer approach for a first product. It limits how much you lose on storage and markdowns if the product doesn't sell as expected, and it gives you real data on demand before you commit more money.
