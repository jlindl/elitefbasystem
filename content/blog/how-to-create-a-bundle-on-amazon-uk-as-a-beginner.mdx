If you want to know how to create a bundle on Amazon UK, the short version is this: you take two or more existing products, usually from physical shops, online directories or wholesale suppliers, and sell them together as a new multi-pack or set that gets its own listing. You are not manufacturing anything. You are combining what already exists into something a shopper would buy as one item, and listing it under your own generic brand using Amazon's GTIN exemption so you don't need a barcode from the original manufacturer.

This is one of the more realistic starting points for a beginner with a few hundred pounds of savings and no history of selling online. You are not betting everything on a brand new product idea. You are testing whether a sensible combination of things people already buy will sell as a set, with a small batch, before you commit any real money to scaling it.

## What a bundle is and why beginners start here

A bundle is a distinct product on Amazon with its own listing, its own images and its own price, even though the individual items inside it might be sold separately elsewhere, by you or by someone else. A kitchen bundle might combine a chopping board, a set of tongs and a bottle of oil. A baby bundle might combine a muslin cloth, a dummy clip and a small toy. The combination has to make sense to a buyer: it should solve a problem or suit an occasion, not just be three unrelated things shrink-wrapped together.

Beginners are drawn to bundling for a few reasons. You can start with retail or wholesale pricing rather than negotiating a manufacturing run. You can test a small batch, see if it sells, and only reorder what has actually moved. And because you are combining existing products rather than inventing one, there is less guesswork about whether the thing itself will work or whether customers will like it. ## How to create a bundle on Amazon UK: the steps from idea to listing

The process has a logical order. Skipping steps, or doing them out of order, is where most early mistakes happen.

### Finding a bundle opportunity

Start by looking at what sells well on its own and asking whether a sensible companion product would make it more appealing, or whether two or three items that are usually bought together could be sold as one convenient set. Walk the aisles of a supermarket, a homeware shop or a pound shop with this question in mind. Check what similar bundles already exist on Amazon, what they charge, and how they are reviewed. If every similar bundle has a poor rating because the contents feel random or cheap, that tells you something about what not to do, not that the category is hopeless.

### Sourcing the parts

Once you have an idea, work out where you can buy the individual items at a price that still leaves room for Amazon's fees, shipping and your own margin, after you have checked the current figures on Amazon's own fee pages. Wholesale suppliers and trade directories are usually cheaper per unit than retail shops once you are buying in any quantity, but retail is a reasonable way to buy a first small test batch without committing to a large order. If any of the products you want to include are branded, check whether that brand requires approval to sell, or whether you need invoices from an authorised supplier. Some categories are gated on Amazon and you will need to apply before you can list in them at all, so check this before you buy stock, not after.

### Listing under a generic brand with a GTIN exemption

Most manufacturers don't supply a barcode for a bundle you have created yourself, because the bundle doesn't exist as a product in their own system. Amazon handles this through a GTIN exemption, which lets you list a product without a manufacturer barcode, usually under a generic brand name while you are starting out. You apply for the exemption in Seller Central for the specific category, and you'll need images and other details that make clear what the bundle actually contains. Amazon has its own policy on what counts as a valid bundle, including rules about how many items you can combine and how the listing must be presented, so check the current bundling policy in Seller Central Help before you create the listing rather than guessing. Policies change, and a listing that doesn't meet them can be removed or flagged.

### Preparing and packing the bundle

Each individual unit inside the bundle still needs its own labels and warnings, even once everything is combined and polybagged together as one set. You will need to think about how the outer packaging holds the items securely for transit, what weight the finished package comes to, and whether it falls into Amazon's rules for heavy or oversized packages. Then you create an FBA shipment in Seller Central, box it up correctly, and track it through to Amazon's fulfilment centre. This is the part of the process where a lot of the practical detail lives: which labels go where, how to pack an outer box so nothing shifts, and how to read the shipment status once it's on its way.

## Checks to make before you list

Before you commit to a batch size, run through this list:

- Does the bundle make obvious sense to a buyer in a few seconds, without you having to explain it
- Have you checked current bundling policy in Seller Central Help, including any limits on how many units or SKUs you can combine
- Are any of the branded items inside the bundle gated, or do they need invoices from an authorised supplier
- Have you worked out your landed cost per unit, including Amazon's fees at their current rate, not an old figure you remember from somewhere
- Is the category you're listing in one you're already approved to sell in, or do you need to apply first
- Have you planned the labelling and polybagging so each individual item still carries the warnings it needs

## Common mistakes beginners make with bundles

The most common mistake is ordering too much stock before anything has sold. A bundle is meant to be tested cheaply. Order a small batch, see how it moves, then reorder based on what actually happened rather than what you hoped would happen.

The second is combining items that don't genuinely belong together. A buyer can tell the difference between a thoughtful set and three unrelated products bundled to hit a price point, and that shows up in reviews.

The third is skipping the policy check. Listing a bundle that doesn't meet Amazon's current rules, or including a branded item you're not approved to sell, risks a suppression notice or worse at exactly the point you're trying to build momentum. Never try to work around this with review swaps, incentivised reviews or a second seller account if something goes wrong: these all risk the account itself.

The fourth is underestimating the packing and labelling work. Each unit inside the bundle still needs to meet its own requirements, and an outer box that isn't packed properly can mean damaged stock arriving at the fulfilment centre.

## What to do next

If this is your first attempt at a bundle, keep the batch small enough that getting it wrong doesn't hurt. Treat it as a test of the model rather than a sure thing: how a bundle performs depends on the products, the pricing and the market, and nobody can tell you the outcome before you've tried it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a barcode to list a bundle on Amazon UK?

Not necessarily. If you're combining products into a new multi-pack or set, Amazon's GTIN exemption lets you list without a manufacturer barcode in many categories, usually under a generic brand name while you're starting out. You apply for the exemption in Seller Central for the specific category and provide images and details that show what the bundle contains.

### Can I bundle branded products from a shop without asking anyone?

Sometimes, but not always. Some brands and categories are gated on Amazon and need approval, or invoices from an authorised supplier, before you can list them at all. Check this before you buy stock, because finding out afterwards means stock you can't list.

### How much stock should a beginner buy for a first bundle?

There's no fixed number, but the principle is to start small. Buy enough to test whether the bundle sells and to see how customers respond, then reorder based on what actually happens rather than committing to a large run before anything has sold.

### Is bundling easier than launching a brand new product?

It's often a gentler starting point because you're combining existing products rather than developing something new, so there's less uncertainty about whether the product itself works. It still carries normal business risk: results depend on the products you choose, the pricing and the market, not on the model alone.
