If you want to know how to send stock to Amazon FBA UK, the short version is this: prepare each unit to Amazon's current rules, pack it into outer boxes that meet their weight and labelling requirements, create a shipment in Seller Central, then track it through to the warehouse until it shows as sellable. That is the whole process in one sentence, but every step has a detail that trips up a first-time seller, and a mistake at any point can mean stock sitting in a reserved state, extra fees, or a shipment that gets refused at the dock.

If you have never sold online before and you are working with a few hundred pounds of savings, this is often the point where Amazon FBA stops feeling theoretical and starts feeling real. You have bought stock, it is sitting in your spare room or garage, and now it has to get into Amazon's system correctly the first time. Here is what that actually involves.

## The steps for sending stock to Amazon

1. **Check each product's prep requirements in Seller Central.** Before you buy a single polybag, look up the exact product on Amazon's seller help pages or inside your shipment plan, because different products need different prep. A loose item might need polybagging, a set of small parts might need to be bundled together, and liquids or fragile items often need extra protection. These rules change, so always check the current version rather than relying on what you read somewhere else.
2. **Polybag units that need it.** Many unboxed or loose items, and almost all bundles made up of more than one piece, need to go into a polybag before they are boxed. If the opening of the bag is a certain size, Amazon requires a printed suffocation warning on the bag itself, not just a sticker added separately. Get this wrong and the shipment can be flagged on arrival.
3. **Apply the correct labels to each unit.** Every unit usually needs a barcode that Amazon can scan, either the product's own barcode or an FNSKU label if you are commingling inventory or using a generic brand under a GTIN exemption. Labels need to be printed clearly, placed where they will not be covered by other packaging, and scannable without the unit being opened.
4. **Pack the outer boxes.** Group units into boxes according to Amazon's box content rules, keep weights within the limits, and make sure boxes are strong enough to survive a courier network. Boxes over a certain weight need a heavy package label on at least one side, and Amazon treats this as a safety rule, not a suggestion, so it is worth weighing boxes properly rather than guessing.
5. **Create the shipment plan in Seller Central.** This is where you tell Amazon what is coming, in what quantities, and from where. Amazon will usually assign the shipment to one or more fulfilment centres, and you choose how you are sending it, whether that is a small parcel carrier or a pallet freight service for larger volumes.
6. **Book the collection or drop-off and print the shipping labels.** Seller Central generates the labels you need for the outer boxes once the shipment is confirmed. These go on the outside of each box, separate from the unit labels inside.
7. **Track the shipment until it shows as received and checked in.** Shipments do not go live the moment they arrive. Amazon has to receive and check them in, and this can take longer at busier times of year. Keep an eye on the shipment status in Seller Central rather than assuming it is done once the courier confirms delivery.

If this is your first shipment, read it through once before you prep anything, because steps two and three are the ones that most often have to be redone.

## How to send stock to Amazon FBA UK: the checks that matter before you ship

A few checks, done before you pack anything, save far more time than fixing problems after the event.

- **Confirm the prep category for your specific product**, not a similar one. Two products that look alike on a shelf can have different prep rules depending on size, material or whether they are sold as a set.
- **Check whether your product needs approval to list at all.** Some categories are gated, and some branded products need invoices from an authorised supplier or specific brand approval before Amazon will let you sell them. This matters a lot if you are sourcing from wholesalers or high street shops rather than manufacturing your own goods, so check this before you commit money to stock.
- **Weigh and measure a sample unit and a sample box** before you commit to a big prep run. If your box is going to come in over a weight limit, you want to know that before you have packed fifty of them.
- **Check the barcode situation.** If you are reselling an existing branded product, you may be able to use its own barcode. If you are building your own bundle under a generic brand, you will likely need a GTIN exemption, which is covered in [Amazon GTIN exemption UK for bundle listings without a barcode](/blog/amazon-gtin-exemption-uk-for-bundle-listings-without-a-barcode).
- **Check your VAT and import position with HMRC or an accountant** if any of your stock comes from outside the UK, since this affects what paperwork you need before stock even reaches a fulfilment centre.

## Common mistakes beginners make

The same handful of mistakes come up again and again with a first shipment.

- **Treating polybagging as optional.** Some sellers skip it to save time or cost, and the shipment gets flagged, or worse, a safety issue is raised later. If the rule says polybag it, polybag it, and print the suffocation warning on the bag if the opening requires one.
- **Printing labels too small or too shiny.** A label that cannot be scanned cleanly causes delays at the warehouse. Use a proper label printer or at minimum good quality adhesive labels, not something squeezed onto a home printer and cut by hand.
- **Guessing box weights instead of weighing them.** A box that looks fine by eye can tip over the heavy package threshold once it is full, and a missing heavy label is a quick way to get a shipment held up.
- **Sending too much stock on a first run.** A beginner who has never sold online before does not yet know how a listing will perform. Sending a large batch before you have proven demand ties up money and increases the damage if the product does not sell as expected. A smaller test batch, checked against real sales, tells you far more than a warehouse full of guesswork.
- **Losing track of the shipment after drop-off.** Shipments can sit in transit or in a receiving queue longer than expected, especially around busy periods. If you assume it is live the day it is delivered, you may list it for sale and disappoint a customer whose order cannot actually be fulfilled yet.
- **Not checking current Amazon policy before each shipment.** Prep and packaging rules are reviewed and changed by Amazon periodically. What worked for your last shipment is not something you can assume will be current for your next one, so check before you prep rather than after a problem is flagged.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need special equipment to prep stock for Amazon FBA?

For most beginners, a decent label printer, polybags in the right sizes, scales, and a tape gun are enough to start. You do not need commercial packing equipment for a small first shipment, but weighing scales that are accurate for both units and full boxes are worth getting right from the start.

### How long does it take for a shipment to go live once it arrives at Amazon?

It varies, and the timing is not fixed by Amazon. Track the shipment status in Seller Central rather than assuming it is live once a courier confirms delivery, and expect longer waits during busier periods of the year.

### Can I send a mixed box with different products in one shipment?

Yes, Amazon allows mixed shipments, but each product still needs to follow its own prep and labelling rules, and boxes need to be organised according to Amazon's current box content rules. Check the specific guidance for mixed shipments in Seller Central before you pack.

### What happens if my shipment gets held up at the warehouse?

Amazon will usually flag the issue in Seller Central, often related to labelling, prep or weight. Check the shipment details for a specific reason, correct it if it is something you can fix, and contact Amazon seller support through the current help channels if the hold is not explained clearly.

### Should I send a large batch of stock on my first shipment?

Most beginners are better off sending a smaller test batch first. This limits how much money is tied up in stock before you know whether a listing actually sells, and it gives you a chance to see how prep, shipping and Amazon's receiving process go in practice before you commit to a bigger run.
