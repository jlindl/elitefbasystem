If you're trying to work out how to find products to sell on Amazon UK, the honest answer is that you don't invent a product out of thin air. You look for a gap between something that already sells well on Amazon and something you can source cheaper, bundled better, or under your own generic brand, then you test it with a small amount of stock before you commit real money to it.

That last part matters more than most beginners expect. With a few hundred pounds of savings and no experience selling online, the risk isn't that FBA is hype. It's that you spend your stock budget on the wrong product because you skipped the checks. This article walks through where to look, what to check before you buy anything, and the mistakes that cost beginners the most.

## Where to actually look for products

Most new sellers assume product research means scrolling an online marketplace looking for inspiration. It's slower and more deliberate than that. There are three main sourcing routes worth learning early.

**Physical shops.** Wholesalers, cash and carries, discount retailers and even well-stocked independent shops often carry products that are selling steadily on Amazon at a higher price, or that could sell well as part of a bundle. Walking the aisles with a phone and a product research tool (or even just the Amazon app to check prices) is a genuine research method, not a shortcut.

**Online wholesale directories.** These list UK wholesalers and distributors by category, with minimum order quantities and trade prices. They're useful once you know roughly what you're looking for, less useful for browsing cold.

**Wholesale suppliers directly.** Once you've identified a product or brand worth stocking, approaching the manufacturer or an authorised distributor directly, often by email, can get you better pricing and access to products you won't find listed publicly. This is also where you start to understand invoices, trade accounts and minimum orders.

Beginners often fixate on finding something nobody else sells. That's rarely how this works. Most viable first products are things already selling on Amazon, which you source more competitively, bundle with a complementary item, or present under your own brand with better packaging or content.

## What makes a product or bundle actually viable

Before you spend anything, run through what a workable product looks like. None of this promises a result, but skipping it is how beginners lose their test budget.

- **Demand already exists.** Products with no sales history on Amazon are a gamble. You want evidence that people are already buying something similar, not a product you personally think is a good idea.
- **The competition is realistic for a beginner.** A category dominated by one or two long-established listings with large numbers of reviews is a hard place to start. Look for categories where several sellers are doing reasonably, not just one giant.
- **The price leaves room for Amazon's fees and your margin.** Amazon charges referral fees, fulfilment fees and storage fees, all of which change over time, so check the current figures in Seller Central or on Amazon's fee pages rather than relying on a number you saw somewhere else. If the maths only works at a thin margin before you've accounted for VAT, shipping and returns, it's too tight.
- **It's light, small and not fragile.** Heavier and bulkier items cost more to ship and store, and anything fragile adds breakage and return risk you don't want on a first product.
- **It isn't gated or brand-restricted in a way you can't clear.** Some categories and brands need approval or supplier invoices before you can list. Check this in Seller Central before you buy stock, not after.
- **A bundle genuinely adds value.** If you're bundling, the combination needs to make sense to a buyer, not just exist to create a new listing. A phone charger and a screen cloth bundled together because they're both small and cheap isn't a bundle, it's two unrelated items in a bag.

## How to find products to sell on amazon uk: validating before you buy

Once you have a shortlist, the next job is proving demand, checking competition and confirming price before any stock gets ordered. This is the step beginners most often rush or skip entirely, usually because it feels slower than just placing an order.

1. **Check what's already selling.** Look at the existing listings for the product or category on Amazon.co.uk. Are there multiple sellers with steady reviews, or is it dominated by one listing with years of history? Either extreme is worth noting.
2. **Check the real price you'd need to sell at.** Look at what similar products or bundles currently sell for, not what you'd like them to sell for. Then work backwards: cost price, Amazon's fees (checked in Seller Central), shipping to Amazon, packaging, and what's left.
3. **Check for restrictions.** Search Seller Central Help for the category and brand to see if approval, gating or invoices are required. If you're planning to sell under a generic brand using a GTIN exemption rather than an existing barcode, it's worth understanding how that works before you build the listing. There's more detail on that in [Amazon GTIN exemption UK for bundle listings without a barcode](/blog/amazon-gtin-exemption-uk-for-bundle-listings-without-a-barcode).
4. **Confirm the supplier terms.** Minimum order quantity, trade price, lead time and whether they'll issue a proper invoice. You'll want that invoice on file if the category or brand ever requires proof of authenticity.
5. **Order a small test batch, not a full pallet.** This is the single biggest difference between beginners who learn steadily and beginners who lose their budget in one go. A small first order tells you whether the product actually sells, whether the listing converts, and whether the supplier is reliable, before you've committed everything you have.

## Common mistakes beginners make

- **Buying stock before checking Amazon's current fees properly.** A product can look profitable on paper and barely break even once fulfilment, referral and storage fees are all accounted for.
- **Choosing a product because it's interesting, not because it sells.** Personal enthusiasm for a niche is not the same as evidence of demand.
- **Skipping the gating check.** Finding out a category needs brand approval after the stock has arrived is an expensive way to learn.
- **Going all in on the first order.** Ordering the maximum the supplier's minimum allows, rather than the smallest viable test quantity, removes your ability to course-correct cheaply.
- **Treating a seller account as something that can't go wrong if you don't think about it.** Suspensions usually come from policy issues, not bad luck, which is why understanding Amazon's seller policies from the start matters more than hoping for the best. If you haven't set the account up yet, it's worth reading about the [difference between an individual and professional seller account](/blog/amazon-individual-vs-professional-seller-account-explained) before you register.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much money do I need to start product research for Amazon FBA?

There's no fixed figure, because it depends on the product and the supplier's minimum order quantity. The principle that matters more than the amount is testing with the smallest viable batch first, rather than committing your full budget to an untested product.

### Can I get my Amazon seller account suspended just for product research?

Research itself won't get you suspended, but what you do with it can. Selling a gated or brand-restricted product without the right approval, or sourcing from someone who can't provide a proper invoice, are common causes. Check Amazon's current policies in Seller Central Help before you list anything you're unsure about.

### Do I need my own brand to start selling on Amazon UK?

No. Many beginners start by reselling or bundling existing products under a generic brand, sometimes using a GTIN exemption rather than a barcode, before moving towards a protected brand of their own as they learn what sells.

### How do I know if a product has too much competition?

Look at how many sellers are doing reasonably well in that category, not just whether competition exists at all. A category with one dominant listing and years of reviews is a harder place to start than one with several moderately successful sellers.

### Is wholesale and bundling a realistic way to start, or should I look at private label first?

Both are used by sellers, and results depend on the product, the work put in and the market at the time. Wholesale and bundling tends to have a lower barrier to entry for a first product, since you're sourcing existing items rather than developing one from scratch, which is why it's often where beginners start.
